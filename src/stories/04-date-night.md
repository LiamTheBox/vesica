# Date Night

Mara had learned something today, and she meant to use it. The rule for date night was simple: go before you leave, go when you arrive, and drink like a person with a plan.

So she went before she left. She went again when she got to the wine bar, a narrow, candlelit place with exposed brick and a chalkboard menu. And she ordered one glass of red, slowly, with a full glass of water beside it, like an adult.

Theo was already there. Theo taught history at the community college, had kind eyes and a laugh that took up the whole room, and on their first date he'd talked for twenty minutes about medieval bridges and somehow made it the best part of her week.

Tonight it was aqueducts.

"The Romans understood water," he said, "better than anyone for a thousand years. Gravity, gradient, pressure. It's all pressure, really."

"You don't say," said Mara.

The wine was good. The water was cold, and the waiter kept quietly refilling it, which was attentive and kind and, she began to realize, a problem. By the time their small plates arrived, she had finished one glass of wine and what felt like three of water.

The restroom was at the back, down a narrow corridor. It was single-occupancy, which she'd noticed on the way in. She'd also noticed the little cluster of people waiting by its door.

She decided to wait for a natural pause in the conversation.

There was no natural pause. Theo had moved from aqueducts to the history of public fountains, and he was so happy telling her about it, and she was so happy listening, that every time she drew breath to say *excuse me for a moment*, he'd say something like, "and here's the wild part," and she'd want to know the wild part.

Under the table, she crossed her legs. Then uncrossed them. Then crossed them the other way.

"Are you cold?" Theo asked.

"Hm? No. Fountains. Tell me about the fountains."

He told her about a fountain in Rome that people threw coins into, thousands of them every day. All that water, rushing and falling and pouring, she thought, was not a helpful image right now. She took a very small sip of wine and set it down, and smiled, and squeezed.

The cluster by the restroom door had shrunk to one person. Then a new couple joined it.

"Theo," she said finally, "hold that thought. Hold the exact thought. I'll be right back."

The line moved with the speed of continental drift. Mara stood with her arms folded and one foot tapping, admiring the exposed brick with great concentration. The door opened. The door closed. Someone inside ran the tap for what felt like a full minute, possibly washing a small dog.

When her turn finally came, she locked the door behind her and closed her eyes and, for a little while, let gravity, gradient and pressure do exactly what the Romans had always known they would.

Back at the table, Theo looked up. "So," he said. "The wild part."

"Tell me everything," said Mara, and meant it.
