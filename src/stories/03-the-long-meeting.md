# The Long Meeting

"Thirty minutes," her boss had promised. "Quick sync. In and out."

Conference room B had no windows and a clock that was clearly lying. The client, a developer named Mr. Hale, had brought two associates, a stack of printouts and the patience of a man who billed by the hour. At minute thirty, he opened the printouts.

Mara sat with her legs crossed at the ankle, her pen poised, her face arranged into what she hoped was *attentive professional* and not *woman doing quiet arithmetic about the cleaning crew*. Twenty minutes, the sign had said. That had been forty minutes ago. The bathroom was surely free. It was sixty steps away. She had counted.

"Could we revisit the lobby?" said Mr. Hale.

They revisited the lobby.

Across the table, her coworker Jun caught her eye. Jun had sat beside her through four years of quick syncs and could read her the way other people read weather. He raised one eyebrow, a millimeter. She gave him a look that meant *don't*.

He didn't. Not then.

An associate passed around bottles of water. Mara took one to be polite and set it down, unopened, at arm's length, as if it might go off. She shifted in her chair. The chair, a sleek Scandinavian thing that had cost more than her rent, creaked like an old ship. Everyone looked at her.

"Great chairs," Mara said.

Mr. Hale nodded slowly. "We should talk about the chairs."

They talked about the chairs.

Somewhere around the second hour, the pressure stopped being a feeling and became a place she lived. It came in waves, rising and fading. She learned to see them coming, a slow swell she could meet with a quiet squeeze and a long breath, and she learned that if she tightened at the start of a wave, it passed sooner. It felt like a skill. It felt like the most important skill she had ever had.

Then Mr. Hale made a joke. It wasn't funny, but it was a client's joke, and it was a client's meeting, so everyone laughed. Jun laughed loudest, and on the way out of the laugh, so only she could hear, he murmured, "Great chairs."

Mara bit the inside of her cheek so hard she tasted copper. She did not laugh. A laugh, right now, would have been a catastrophe. She held her breath, held everything, held on, and watched the clock lie for another eleven minutes.

"Well," said Mr. Hale at last, gathering his printouts. "Productive."

Mara stood up very carefully, shook three hands, said *thank you so much* three times, and walked sixty steps in a straight line at a speed that was not technically running.

The sign was gone. The door was open.

Some feelings don't have good words. Mara leaned her forehead against the cool tiles of the stall and let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since 7:52 that morning, and decided that relief, real relief, was underrated as a human experience.

When she came out, Jun was waiting by the water cooler, grinning.

"Never," she said, "say *chairs* to me again."
