# The Road Trip

Jun drove like a man on a mission, and the mission was the coast by sunset.

"No stops," he announced, pulling out of the city in his secondhand hatchback, Priya in the passenger seat with the snacks and Mara in the back with the bags. "We stop, we hit the traffic. We don't stop, we beat the traffic. It's science."

"It's not science," said Priya.

"It's road science," said Jun.

Mara had prepared. She'd gone twice before they left. She'd brought a bottle of water and intended to sip it the way the internet said you should: a little at a time, never too much. Then Priya passed back a giant iced tea from the gas station, because it had been two-for-one, and it was a hot day, and Mara was a person who could be talked into things.

For an hour, it was perfect. Open highway, the windows down, a playlist they'd built together over a decade of friendship. Mara watched farmland roll past and felt the week's knots loosen.

At hour two, the iced tea's bill arrived.

It came on gently, the way it always did, a polite knock from somewhere south of her seat belt. She shifted. She recrossed her legs. She looked at the map on Jun's phone and saw the next services sign: *40 km*.

Forty kilometers. Twenty-five minutes. She could do twenty-five minutes. She'd done a two-hour meeting with a man who wanted to talk about chairs.

At kilometer thirty-two, the traffic stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. Brake lights for a mile in every direction, red and patient and absolutely immovable. Jun groaned. Priya put her feet up on the dashboard. Mara looked at the line of cars ahead of them and felt a wave rise, slow and certain, and met it the way she'd learned, with a long breath out and a squeeze held steady until it passed.

"You're quiet," said Priya, twisting round.

"Meditating," said Mara.

The traffic moved a car length. It stopped again. A detour sign sent them off onto a farm road that had last been paved during some other century. Every bump arrived like a pop quiz. Mara braced for each one, hands flat on the seat, and counted them out loud under her breath, which Jun mistook for her singing along.

"Seven kilometers!" Priya called, reading a sign. "Mara, you're doing great. I know that face."

"There's no face."

"There's a face."

Five kilometers. Three. The services appeared over a hill like a mirage: a gas station, a diner, and a little building with a blue sign that Mara would, she felt, remember for the rest of her life.

Jun pulled in. Mara was out of the car before it had fully stopped, walking with the precise, dignified urgency she had spent the whole week perfecting, and she made it, and the relief was so total and so huge that she laughed out loud, alone, in a gas station restroom in the middle of nowhere.

They reached the coast twenty minutes after sunset. The sky was still pink at the edges. Jun bought fries, Priya found a bench, and Mara sat on the seawall with her shoes off and the sea breathing in and out below her.

"Good week?" Jun asked.

Mara thought about the coffee, the commute, the meeting, the wine bar, the festival and forty very long kilometers.

"A week of holding on," she said. "I got really good at it."

She took a sip of her water: a small one, perfectly timed.
